# Preface {.unnumbered}

This book sets out to fix one specific kind of discomfort.

Open a browser today and you will run into the same handful of words: large model, compute, open source, parameters, agents. Each of them has an explanation somewhere, but nobody tells you how they relate to each other. So you end up in an awkward spot — you know these things are changing the world, yet you cannot say how the change is happening, still less which road leads into a wall.

That leaves two familiar kinds of book. One is popular science: you finish it knowing the story, but you still cannot judge anything. The other is a textbook: it asks you to work through three chapters of maths first. This book takes a third road. It does not teach you to calculate; it teaches you to judge. Every technical approach gets the same four questions — what is it trying to solve, why does it work, what does it cost, and when does it break.

The fourth question matters most, and it is the one most often left out. When a technology is introduced, its strengths are always crisp and its weaknesses blur into "there are still some limitations". This book does not accept that blur. Every weakness has to be derivable from the underlying principle, not copied from a news article.

The book has two parts. Part one covers artificial intelligence, from a single artificial neuron up to agents, and along the way sets the three main architecture families of large models side by side so you can see their trade-offs. Part two covers software development, from "what is a program" to licence politics, including version control, dependencies, reproducibility and declarative systems. You can read the two parts separately; together they make one complete picture, because the model is the most conspicuous layer of this system but not the only one.

Formulas do appear in the book, but only in the "Maths note" at the end of each section. Skip them and the main text still stands on its own; if you want to go deeper, they are there.
